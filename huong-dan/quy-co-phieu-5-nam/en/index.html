How Vietnam's equity funds did over five years, and why

A step-by-step study of 14 open-ended funds and three offshore funds, from the end of 2021 to September 2026

Updated 13 Nov 2026 · data to 30 Sep 2026 · sources: Fmarket, fund managers' factsheets

This is a long note. It is written as a guide: each step says what I measured, where the number comes from, and what could be wrong with it. If you only want the answer, read the next section and stop.

The short answer

  • From the end of 2021 to 30 September 2026 the VN-Index returned about 18%. Ten of the 14 open-ended equity funds with a full record did better. The median fund returned 21%.
  • The whole lead was earned in 2022, 2023 and 2024. In those three years nearly every fund beat the index. In 2025 none of 19 did.
  • The reason is one group of companies. The funds owned almost no Vingroup shares at the start of 2025. Those shares then made about three-quarters of the index's 41% rise.
  • Before that, the funds had done two things well. They owned little real estate when it collapsed in 2022, and they owned a lot of FPT, Mobile World and private banks when those led the market in 2023-24.
  • After that, they did one more thing badly. Many held FPT at 10-20% of the fund into 2025, when it fell.
  • The offshore funds listed in London did worse than the local funds, and the index-tracking funds built on foreign indexes did worst of all until 2025.

What is being measured

The funds. I use every open-ended Vietnamese equity fund that the fund platform Fmarket lists and that has a published price for each day I need. Fourteen have a record covering the whole period. Five more started later and appear only in the year-by-year counts. One further fund is left out because I have a connection to its sponsor.

The price. An open-ended fund publishes a net asset value per unit, the NAV. It is the value of everything the fund owns, after fees, divided by the number of units. The change in NAV is what an investor earned, before any entry or exit fee. Dividends the fund receives stay inside the NAV.

The yardstick. Every fund here compares itself with the VN-Index, so I do too. The index returns in this note are the ones the fund managers print in their own factsheets: -32.8% in 2022, +12.2% in 2023, +12.1% in 2024, +40.9% in 2025 and -0.9% from January to September 2026. Multiplied together they give +18.0%.

The period. End of 2021 to 30 September 2026, which is four years and nine months. The start matters. The market peaked in early January 2022, so this period begins almost exactly at a top. Every return here would look better from a start one year later and worse from a start one year earlier, when the funds had just had their best year: the managers' factsheets show 2021 returns of 38% to 67% for these funds against 35.7% for the index.

Ten of 14 are ahead of the index. That sounds like a good result for active management. Two adjustments make it less so.

Dividends. The VN-Index is a price index. It leaves out dividends, and the funds keep theirs. VinaCapital's factsheet for December 2024 puts the index's dividend yield at 2.2%. Adding about two points a year lifts the index's return to roughly 28-30%. Only three funds are clearly above that, and two more sit on the line.

Survivors. The list is the funds that exist today. A fund that did badly and was closed or merged is not in it. That flatters the group, by an amount I cannot measure.

Three things stand out.

One stock sat on top of almost every fund at the end of 2024. FPT was the largest holding in five of the six funds above, at 9% to 14% of NAV. SSISCA had held nearly 20% two years earlier, which is the legal maximum for a single company.

The names at the top changed completely in one year. By the end of 2025 FPT had dropped to 6% or less of these funds, and at Dragon Capital the two Vingroup companies had gone from nothing to the top of the list.

Banks are now the core of every portfolio. In the latest factsheets banks are the largest sector in all six funds, at 28% to 44% of NAV.

The second useful table is the gap between a fund and the index in one sector. VinaCapital and SSIAM print the index's sector weights beside their own, so the gap can be read directly. Real estate is the sector that mattered.

Step 4: what went right

2022: they lost less. The index fell 32.8%. SSIAM's year-end report puts the real-estate sector's fall at 46%. A fund with 3% in real estate instead of 18% missed most of that. VESAF also held 24% of the fund in cash at the end of 2022. Fourteen of 15 funds finished ahead of the index, and four lost only 13% to 17%.

2023-24: they owned what led. Dragon Capital's 2024 report lists the year's winners in its DCDS fund: FPT up 84.9%, Techcombank up 59.9%, Mobile World up 43.7%, VietinBank up 39.5%. These were the funds' largest positions. SSISCA beat the index by 17 points in 2023 and 21 points in 2024.

2025: a few changed their minds in time. DCDS returned 31% in 2025, the second-best result in the group, because it bought Vingroup and Vinhomes during the year and raised cash before the tariff shock in April. It still finished behind the index.

Step 5: what went wrong

They did not own the stock that drove the index. The managers' own words are the clearest evidence.

  • Dragon Capital, on DCDS: Vingroup-related stocks added nearly 400 points to the index in 2025, more than 75% of its rise.
  • SSIAM: without those stocks the index rose 12.6%, and more than half of all listed shares ended the year lower.
  • VinaCapital: its open-ended funds held no Vingroup or Gelex shares, which made about three-quarters of the index's gain.
  • VinaCapital's London-listed fund: "We are fundamental investors and do not own VIC." Its report says VIC rose 736% in the year.

A fund that returned 12% in 2025 was in line with the average stock. It was 29 points behind the index.

They held the previous winner too long. FPT was 10% to 14% of several funds at the end of 2024. VinaCapital's London fund reports that the share fell 26% in 2025 and was its main drag. The funds with the most FPT at the end of 2024 were the furthest behind in 2025: SSISCA by 29 points, VESAF by 34.

Some started 2022 with too much steel and property. This applies to the largest funds more than the small ones. Dragon Capital's London-listed fund held 12% in Hoa Phat and 27% in real estate in early 2022. DCDS fell 34.5% that year, more than the index.

In 2026 several chased. VinaCapital's London fund first bought VIC in the third quarter of 2026, after refusing it through all of 2025. DCDS reports a loss of 13.2% for the nine months to September against 0.9% for the index, and its report does not explain the gap.

Step 6: could they have owned it?

Partly. Vietnamese rules limit how concentrated an open-ended fund can be. As set out in Article 35 of Circular 98/2020 (I read a law firm's transcription, not the official text):

  • no more than 20% of the fund in one company;
  • no more than 30% in one group of related companies;
  • all positions of 5% or more, added together, no more than 40% of the fund.

Vingroup alone was 15.7% of the index at the end of 2025, going by the figures in VEIL's report, and real estate as a whole was 27%. A fund could not have matched those weights without breaking the third rule or giving up nearly every other large position. So part of the 2025 shortfall was built into the rules.

But the rules allowed far more than zero. DCDS held 10.4% in Vingroup and 8.3% in Vinhomes at the end of 2025 and stayed inside the limits. Most funds held none because they judged the price too high and the debt too heavy. That was a choice, and in 2025 it was the wrong one for anyone measured against the index.

Multiplying VEIL's figures gives a loss of about 10% over the period, against a gain of about 13% for its benchmark. The same pattern shows as at home, with two extra costs.

Private deals. VOF holds unlisted investments. Two instruments linked to the developer Novaland defaulted on 30 December 2022 and were written down by US$19.9m.

The discount. Shares in a London-listed fund trade below NAV. VEIL's discount moved between 11% and 21% over the period, so a shareholder's return also depended on the day they bought and sold. Both boards faced votes on whether the funds should continue and responded with share buybacks, tender offers and lower fees.

PYN Elite is the exception in style. It runs a very concentrated portfolio: at the end of 2022 its two largest holdings were 34% of the fund, and at the end of 2024 one bank was 20.9%. That helped in 2024 and hurt in 2023, 2025 and 2026.

Step 8: the index funds, for contrast

Funds that track an index made the opposite trade without deciding anything. Their top holdings today show it. The VN30 fund holds 11.4% in Vingroup. The US-listed VanEck fund holds Vingroup, Vinhomes and Masan as its three largest positions. The Diamond fund, which tracks an index of stocks that foreigners can no longer buy more of, holds 14% in FPT and 15.6% in Mobile World and no Vingroup at all.

So the index funds built on broad indexes owned the 2025 winner by default, and the Diamond fund owned the 2023-24 winners by default and then their fall. Taiwan's exchange shows a five-year return of 5.7% for the Fubon Vietnam fund in Taiwan dollars. Finhay's page for the Diamond fund shows 15.7% over five years to late October 2026. Both are below the median active fund's 21%, though the dates differ a little.

What I take from it

  1. Measure a manager over more than one kind of market. Every fund here looked skilled through 2024 and unskilled in 2025. One portfolio style produced both results.
  2. Find the one position that explains the gap. For these funds it was real estate in 2022 and 2025, and one technology company in between. Sector tables in the factsheets show it faster than any return figure.
  3. A fund that avoids the largest company in the index is making a large bet. It may be a sensible bet. It should be judged as one.
  4. Check the rules before blaming the manager. Concentration limits explain part of 2025, though not all of it.
  5. Dividends and dead funds both flatter the comparison. Add about two points a year to the index and remember the list contains survivors only.

How to repeat this study

Everything here comes from public pages.

  1. List the funds. Fmarket's equity-fund page shows each fund, its manager and its latest NAV.
  2. Get the NAV history. Each fund page has the full price history. Take the last price on or before each date you need.
  3. Calculate returns. Divide the later NAV by the earlier one and subtract one. Do it for each calendar year and for the whole period.
  4. Check against the factsheet. Each manager prints calendar-year returns. If yours differ by more than a point, find out why before going on.
  5. Get the index returns. Use the ones printed in the same factsheets, so both sides of the comparison come from the same document.
  6. Read the December factsheets. Write down the top holdings, the sector weights, the cash level and what the manager says helped or hurt.
  7. Line the two up. For each year, ask which holdings explain the gap to the index.

Three traps cost me time.

  • Distributions. DCDE pays cash to its holders. Its NAV drops on those days and the price history does not adjust for it. My first calculation showed a loss of 19% for a fund whose own factsheets imply about 3%. The check against the factsheet is what caught it.
  • Weekly prices. Some funds publish a NAV once a week. Around a sharp fall, such as April 2025, a weekly fund can look far ahead of or behind the index for a few days.
  • Factsheets that print only five holdings. A stock missing from the top five may still be 4% of the fund. Use the sector table to check.

What this study does not show

  • It covers 14 funds on one platform. Some large funds are not sold there.
  • Holdings are year-end snapshots. They do not show what was bought and sold in between.
  • I could not find factsheets for 2021-23 for the Dragon Capital funds, or for the end of 2024 for SSISCA.
  • The period starts at a market peak. Other periods would give other rankings.
  • Nothing here says what these funds will do next.
