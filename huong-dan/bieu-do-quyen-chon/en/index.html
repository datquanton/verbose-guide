Option payoff charts, built step by step

An animated chart for six option strategies: build each piece, slide by the premium, then read the breakeven, maximum gain and maximum loss

Updated 23 Nov 2026 · illustrative numbers, not market prices · source: standard option payoffs at expiry

Key points

  • The horizontal axis is the share price on the last day (expiry), not time. The vertical axis is your profit or loss. Each point on the line answers one question: “if the share ends at this price, how much do I make or lose?”
  • Slopes give the shape, and the line only bends at a strike. A share has slope +1 everywhere; a bought call has slope +1 to the right of its strike; a bought put has slope −1 to the left. Writing an option flips the signs.
  • The premium gives the height: pay it and the whole line slides down, receive it and the line slides up. The breakeven is where the line crosses zero.

1 · Watch the chart build itself

Pick a strategy. The chart builds itself in seven scenes, then a dot runs along the price axis while the profit sum updates beside it. Drag the slider to move the price yourself.

2 · How to read the chart

  1. Pick a last-day price on the horizontal axis.
  2. Go up (or down) to the blue line.
  3. Look left to read your profit or loss.

The dashed lines are only markers: S₀ (what you paid for the share) and K (the strike, where the line bends). S₀ is never a bend: the share has slope +1 everywhere.

3 · Draw any strategy in four steps

  1. Mark the prices: S₀ if you own the share, and every strike.
  2. Draw the shape: add up each piece’s slope in each zone between strikes.
  3. Slide by the premium: paid means down, received means up.
  4. Read the answers: crossing zero is the breakeven; the flat top is the maximum gain; the flat bottom is the maximum loss.

Method and sources

Profit is per share at expiry, ignoring the time value of money. A call is worth max(0, S_T − K) and a put max(0, K − S_T). Profit = value of the pieces on the last day − premiums paid + premiums received. All numbers are illustrative, with the share priced around 100; they are not market prices.
